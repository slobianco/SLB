### Cielo Rojo has been a long journey. What exactly is this record to you

I think it started as an album and gradually became something more personal than that.

There are eleven songs, but they weren't written because I decided one day that I needed eleven
tracks for a record. They appeared at different moments of my life and, eventually, I realised
there was a thread connecting them.

Memory. Time. Anxiety. People who are no longer where they once were. The person you used to be.
The person you become.

At some point I stopped seeing them as separate songs and started seeing Cielo Rojo as one
journey.

### Why call it Cielo Rojo

The red sky became an image that somehow contained the whole record for me.

There's something beautiful about it, but there's also something slightly unsettling. A red sky
can feel like the end of something or the beginning of something.

That's very close to what the album is about.

You're moving forwards, but you're constantly carrying pieces of what came before.

### There are eleven tracks. Is there a deliberate story running through them

Not a literal story where track two explains track one and track three tells you what happens
next.

It's more emotional than that.

Silent Memory begins in a place of memory and absence. Songs such as Review Yourself, Your Ghost
In Me and London Darkened Roads look at identity, relationships and what we carry with us.

Cielo Rojo sits almost at the centre of the record.

Then you have songs like The Quest, This Is Not Me, Raining in Sydney and There Is No Time, where
the perspective starts changing.

By the time you reach La Desolación and Ansiedad, I don't think the record is necessarily giving
you an answer.

But you've travelled somewhere.

That's important to me.

### What should someone who has never heard your music expect

Guitars.

Quite a lot of guitars, actually.

But I don't think it's an album that fits comfortably into one box. There are elements of rock,
alternative rock, punk, progressive music, metal and more melodic things.

Some parts are aggressive. Some are very quiet. Some songs are immediate and others take their
time.

The common element isn't really the genre. It's the emotional atmosphere.

I wanted the record to feel cinematic without losing the feeling of people actually playing music.

### You've described Cielo Rojo as a one-man-band project. How literal is that

Pretty literal, although I don't want that description to become some sort of heroic statement
about doing absolutely everything alone.

The songs, lyrics, melodies, guitars, bass, arrangements and creative direction are mine. I've
spent an enormous amount of time recording, changing things, throwing things away and then
occasionally putting the thing I threw away back again.

Modern production technology has also been part of the process.

For me, the interesting thing isn't whether every sound came into existence in exactly the same
way.

The question is whether, when everything comes together, it says what I wanted it to say.

### Technology and AI have become part of modern music-making. How did you approach that

As tools.

That's probably the simplest answer.

Technology helped me explore ideas and sometimes achieve things that would have been difficult for
me to achieve alone. But it didn't decide what these songs were about. It didn't live the
experiences behind them. It didn't write the lyrics or decide which idea was worth keeping.

I've also deliberately brought more of my own guitar and bass playing into the final recordings as
the project evolved.

> The technology should serve the record.
>
> The record shouldn't exist to demonstrate the technology.

### Was making Cielo Rojo partly about returning to the musician you used to be

Interestingly, I think I started believing that.

But somewhere during the process I realised I didn't actually want to recreate that person.

I'd already made music before. I was part of Sonnar and we released Frágil. That belongs to
another very important period of my life.

Cielo Rojo couldn't be another version of that.

Too much time had passed.

So eventually the question changed from Can I still do this to What does making music mean to me
now.

That's a much more interesting question.

### Is there one song that explains the album better than the others

Probably not.

And I'm glad there isn't.

Cielo Rojo obviously carries the name of the album, but something like Silent Memory reveals
another side of it. The Quest asks different questions again. Raining in Sydney occupies its own
emotional space.

I think if one song could explain everything, I probably wouldn't have needed the other ten.

### How popular do you expect the album to become

I genuinely have no idea.

And I think trying to predict that would be a very efficient way of ruining the experience.

I'd obviously love people to listen to it. I'd love someone I've never met, somewhere I've never
been, to hear one of these songs and feel that it belongs to them somehow.

But I'm not measuring the value of Cielo Rojo by imagining a number of streams before it has even
been released.

For a long time this album existed in my head, on hard drives, inside unfinished Cakewalk projects
and in an increasingly ridiculous number of versions.

> Finishing it already means something.
>
> Anything beyond that is where the listener takes over.

### What do you hope listeners are left with when the final track ends

A question, perhaps.

Not necessarily the same question I had when I wrote it.

There are things in the album that are very personal to me, but I don't want to explain every one
of them. Once somebody listens to a song, their own memories become part of it.

That's where music becomes more interesting.

Cielo Rojo isn't an attempt to recreate who I was when I first started making music.

It's a record about who remained after everything changed.
